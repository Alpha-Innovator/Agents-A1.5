3. CHALLENGE DOSSIERS

Challenge P — Returned organics and contamination

Claim-specific evidence matrix:

Why three propositions are distinct:

  1. Molecule detection — analytical identification (e.g., glycine signal in Stardust foils). Cannot distinguish extraterrestrial from terrestrial because these molecules are ubiquitous on Earth.
  2. Extraterrestrial provenance — positive evidence the molecule formed in the parent-body environment: isotopic signatures outside terrestrial ranges, racemic chirality (biology produces homochiral L-amino acids), structural isomers rare in biology (6-methyluracil), violation of Chargaff's rule, spatial localization (comet-exposed side only), absence in procedural blanks. All three missions used combinations of these tests.
  3. Biological origin — requires evidence of life processes (metabolism, replication, enzymatic catalysis). None of these missions can establish this; all findings point to abiotic synthesis: racemic mixtures prove non-biological formation; heavy isotope enrichment reflects cold molecular cloud or parent-body chemistry. Finding building blocks ≠ finding life.

Remaining limitations: Ryugu nucleobase MS/MS confirmed only for guanine/cytosine in one sample; Bennu has 1.24% sample escape contamination; Stardust glycine δ¹³C is a lower limit; β-alanine cometary origin unconfirmed (insufficient material).

Strongest abiotic accounts:

  • Enceladus H₂: serpentinization favored (silica nanoparticles >90°C require hydrothermal conditions; Hsu et al. 2015), but rock-grinding and radiolysis are alternatives.
  • 67P O₂: Yao & Giapis 2017's Eley-Rideal mechanism explains (a) strong O₂–H₂O correlation, (b) near-nucleus enhancement, (c) HO₂/O₂ ratio ~3× interstellar, (d) absence of O₃ — which is problematic for primordial radiolysis theory.
  • 67P glycine: radical addition on interstellar ice mantles at 40–120 K; no aqueous alteration needed.

Discriminating observations:

  • Enceladus: clumped-isotope analysis (Δ¹³CH₃D) would distinguish biotic vs. abiotic methane; isotopic D/H and ¹³C/¹²C in CH₄; chirality of any amino acids.
  • 67P O₂: temporal variability correlated with solar wind flux supports in-situ production; spatial mapping of O₂ distribution distinguishes point-source vs. uniform.
  • Neither Earth analogy (hydrothermal vents, interstellar chemistry) constitutes direct observation of extraterrestrial microbes.

Methodological differences driving disagreement:

  • Wavelength coverage: NIR (0.9–5.2 μm) constrains CH₄/CO₂; MIRI (5–12 μm) is where DMS/DMDS strongest features lie; joint panchromatic (Luque 2025) shows DMS preference in MIR depends on fitting NIR correctly.
  • Retrieval frameworks: AURA (Madhusudhan group) vs. petitRADTRANS/TauREx (Luque) vs. POSEIDON/BeAR (Schmidt) yield different posterior preferences.
  • Cross-section libraries: HITRAN DMS/DMDS measured at STP (1 bar, 298K, N₂ broadener) may be inaccurate under K2-18b conditions (high-pressure H₂-rich atmosphere).
  • Model assumptions: Hycean (requires biogenic CH₄ against photochemical destruction) vs. mini-Neptune (thermochemical quenching naturally produces CH₄). Both fit data with χ²ᵣ ≈ 1.51.
  • Red noise/systematics: Stevenson et al. 2025 find MIRI data susceptible to binning-dependent systematics; 87.5% of retrievals with preferred binning do not support DMS.

Comparison with returned-sample claims (Bennu/Ryugu): Exoplanet transmission spectroscopy follows spectral feature → molecule ID → significance → habitable environment → biosignature inference, but lacks the multi-step provenance testing (isotopes, chirality, contamination checks, procedural blanks) that sample-return missions use to distinguish extraterrestrial from terrestrial and abiotic from biotic. A single spectral feature at 3σ is a fundamentally weaker evidentiary foundation than a molecule whose extraterrestrial provenance is established by multiple independent isotopic, chirality, and contamination-control tests.

Resolution: As of Sept 2026 cutoff, K2-18b remains genuinely unresolved: CH₄ robustly detected; CO₂ likely present but contested; DMS/DMDS unconfirmed; Hycean vs. mini-Neptune interpretation unsettled. ~25 additional MIRI transits estimated (Luque 2025) for decisive test. NASA's position: "detection of a single potential biosignature would not constitute discovery of life."

Generalization dependencies:

  • Object identity: DART tested a ~160 m secondary moonlet of a binary; a single asteroid or larger body responds differently.
  • Structure: Dimorphos is rubble-pile (porosity ~35%, cohesion < few Pa); monolithic/S-type bodies yield different β and fragmentation behavior.
  • Geometry: Head-on leading-hemisphere collision maximized transfer; different angles change outcome.
  • Measurement time: Mutual orbit measured over months; heliocentric required 3+ years of astrometry for μm/s detection.
  • Model dependence: β derived from Monte Carlo over 12 unknown parameters; Lucy formation hypotheses depend on assumed YORP strength and collisional models.

Cannot generalize: The 33-min period change and β = 2.2–4.9 are specific to Dimorphos's size, rubble-pile structure, and impact geometry. Warning time of years-to-decades is necessary for planetary defense applications. Lucy's morphological conclusions apply to its specific targets (main-belt S-type ~720–790 m), not to DART-like NEO binaries without separate justification.

Arrokoth (bilobed shape):

  • Observation: ~35 km contact binary, lobes aligned within 5°, flattened lenticular shapes, low crater count.
  • Inferred: gravitational collapse of overdense pebble cloud → gentle merger preserving pristine shape.
  • Competing: hierarchical accretion via catastrophic collisions; spin instability/fission; random collision.
  • Discriminating: alignment unlikely from random collision; impacts at mutual escape velocity (~m/s) would be destructive; uniform composition indicates locally sourced material; NASA concludes "all but rule out hierarchical accretion" for Arrokoth specifically.
  • Key point: Formation scenario constrained but formation event not directly observed.

Do not infer: Liquid composition or present-day interior directly from visual resemblance. Channels on Titan look water-like but are methane; Pluto's young terrain suggests activity but ocean is entirely inferred.

5. PUBLICATION-READY PANEL SET (Corrected Exhibition Text)

Panel A — Hayabusa2: "Japan's Hayabusa2 spacecraft collected samples from asteroid Ryugu and returned them to Earth in a capsule that landed at Woomera, Australia on 6 December 2020. Analysis revealed organic compounds including amino acids and nucleobases — but these are racemic (equal right- and left-handed forms) products of abiotic chemistry, not evidence of past life. The spacecraft survived its sample drop-off and continues an Extended Mission."

Panel B — OSIRIS-REx: "NASA's OSIRIS-REx delivered Bennu samples to Utah on 24 September 2023 — the first asteroid sample return by the United States, following Japan's earlier successes. January 2025 analyses identified 14 of the 20 amino acids used by terrestrial life and all five DNA/RNA nucleobases in the Bennu material. These are molecular building blocks, not intact genetic material; the researchers explicitly stated the findings 'do not show evidence for life itself.'"

Panel C — DART: "On 26 September 2022, NASA's DART spacecraft struck the moonlet Dimorphos (not its parent asteroid Didymos) at ~22,530 km/h, shortening Dimorphos's orbit around Didymos by 33.0 ± 1.0 minutes — a proof-of-concept for kinetic impact deflection. The same impact altered the binary system's orbit around the Sun by only 11.7 ± 1.3 micrometers per second. NASA emphasizes that sufficient warning time (years to decades) is essential and that applicability depends on target size, structure, and geometry."

Panel D — Lucy: "NASA's Lucy spacecraft flew by main-belt asteroid Dinkinesh on 1 November 2023 — its first asteroid encounter, but not a Jupiter Trojan. The mission discovered that Dinkinesh's satellite Selam is a contact binary, the first such object found orbiting an asteroid. Lucy's Jupiter Trojan encounters begin with Eurybates in August 2027; main-belt targets served as practice runs."

Panel E — Juice: "ESA's Juice launched 14 April 2023 and is planned to arrive at Jupiter on 21 July 2031. It will study Ganymede (primary target), Callisto, and Europa through flybys and by entering orbit around Ganymede. Juice has no landing capability and is an entirely separate mission from NASA's Europa Clipper."

Panel F — Europa Clipper: "NASA's Europa Clipper launched 14 October 2024. Its goal is to assess whether Europa could support life — measuring ice shell thickness, ocean composition, and surface geology. It arrives at Jupiter in April 2030 and begins Europa flybys in Spring 2031. The spacecraft carries no life-detection instruments, drill, or landing system, and has not yet reached its target."

Panel G — Stardust: "NASA's Stardust flew through comet Wild 2's coma and captured dust in aerogel panels, returning the capsule to Earth on 15 January 2006. Glycine — a single amino acid, one building block of proteins — was identified in the collector; its extraterrestrial origin was confirmed by carbon-isotope ratios (+29±6‰ vs. terrestrial −6 to −40‰). No lander was involved."

Panel H — Rosetta/Philae: "ESA's Rosetta orbiter released the Philae lander onto comet 67P on 12 November 2014; harpoon failure caused Philae to bounce before settling. Neither vehicle returned samples to Earth — the original sample-return design was dropped for budget reasons. ROSINA detected molecular oxygen and glycine in the comet's coma, and COSAC found 16 organic compounds on the surface — all prebiotic chemicals, not evidence of biology."

Panel I — Cassini: "Cassini's INMS detected ~1% molecular hydrogen in Enceladus's plume during an October 2015 flyby, indicating hydrothermal water-rock reactions producing chemical free energy — conditions 'in a similar way to' Earth's vents. A 2023 analysis of archival CDA data found phosphate concentrations hundreds of times Earth's oceans. Cassini was not able to detect life and found no evidence of inhabitation. The mission ended 15 September 2017 with controlled entry into Saturn's atmosphere."

Panel J — Huygens: "ESA's Huygens probe landed on Titan, Saturn's largest moon, on 14 January 2005 — the farthest successful landing from Earth. It imaged drainage channels carved by flowing liquid methane (not water) and detected a methane signal increase suggesting surface liquid. Titan's lakes, discovered by Cassini radar in July 2006, contain methane and ethane."

Panel K — New Horizons: "NASA's New Horizons flew past Pluto on 14 July 2015 at ~7,800 km altitude — a flyby, not an orbit. It then traveled to fly past Arrokoth (486958), an independent Kuiper Belt Object, on 1 January 2019. Arrokoth's bilobed shape constrains planetesimal formation models (gravitational collapse of a pebble cloud) but does not directly observe the formation event. Pluto's subsurface ocean remains an inference from surface observations and thermal models."

Panel L — JWST/K2-18b: "JWST transmission spectroscopy robustly detected methane (~4–5σ) and carbon dioxide (~3σ, contested) in exoplanet K2-18b's atmosphere. A tentative dimethyl sulfide detection (~2.7–3.2σ) is actively disputed: independent reanalyses find insufficient or no reliable evidence, and abiotic production pathways exist. The planet's nature — Hycean ocean world vs. gas-rich mini-Neptune — remains unresolved. NASA states that a single potential biosignature 'would not constitute discovery of life.'"
